\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We will evaluate the models using quantitative metrics for both reconstruction quality and editing performance, under a few-shot test-time setup.   

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)}

    The Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) is a widely adopted metric for evaluating the quality of images generated by deep generative models, particularly in tasks such as few-shot image generation \cite{heusel2017gans, wang2023attribute}. Rather than comparing images directly at the pixel level, FID operates in a high-level feature space extracted from a pretrained Inception-v3 network \cite{heusel2017gans}. This makes it effective for capturing both the fidelity (realism) and diversity (variety) of generated samples \cite{borji2022pros}. The FID score is computed by modeling the feature distributions of real and generated images as multivariate Gaussians \cite{heusel2017gans}. The FID score is computed by modeling the feature distributions of real and generated images as multivariate Gaussians. The formula for FID is:

    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{FID} = \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\left( \Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\left(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g\right)^{1/2} \right)
        \end{equation}
        
        Where:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\mu_r$, $\Sigma_r$ are the mean and covariance of the Inception features of real images,
            \item $\mu_g$, $\Sigma_g$ are the mean and covariance of the Inception features of generated images,
            \item $\|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2$ represents the squared Euclidean distance between the feature means,
            \item $\mathrm{Tr}(\cdot)$ is the trace operator, which sums the diagonal elements of a matrix,
            \item $(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2}$ denotes the matrix square root of the product of the covariance matrices.
        \end{itemize}
        
    This formula computes the Fréchet (2-Wasserstein) distance between two multivariate Gaussian distributions—one fitted to real images and the other to generated images in the feature space \cite{heusel2017gans}. \textit{Lower FID is better.} Since FID measures the distance between real and generated image distributions, a lower FID score indicates that the generated images are more similar to the real images in terms of both quality and diversity. An FID of 0 implies perfect similarity. Therefore, in few-shot image generation, models that achieve lower FID scores are considered to generate more realistic and varied samples \cite{borji2022pros, wang2023attribute}.

    \item \textbf{Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)}
    
    The Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) is a metric used to evaluate the perceptual similarity between two images. Instead of comparing images at the pixel level, LPIPS compares deep feature activations from a pretrained neural network (such as VGG or AlexNet), which better reflect human perception~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}. This makes LPIPS especially useful in few-shot image generation, where we want to evaluate whether generated images are visually close to target images (for fidelity), or measure how diverse the outputs are (for diversity), depending on how it is applied. The LPIPS score between two images $x$ and $x'$ is calculated as:
    \[
    \text{LPIPS}(x, x') = \sum_{l} \frac{1}{H_l W_l} \sum_{h=1}^{H_l} \sum_{w=1}^{W_l} \left\| w_l \odot \left( \hat{y}^l_{hw}(x) - \hat{y}^l_{hw}(x') \right) \right\|^2
    \]
    where $\hat{y}^l_{hw}(x)$ is the unit-normalized feature vector of image $x$ at layer $l$ and position $(h, w)$, $w_l$ is a vector of learned weights for each channel at layer $l$, and $H_l$, $W_l$ are the height and width of the feature map at layer $l$~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}. When interpreting LPIPS, \textit{lower values are better} in terms of similarity between generated and real images. A lower LPIPS score indicates that the generated image is more perceptually similar to the reference image. On the other hand, a higher LPIPS score may reflect less similarity (in fidelity tasks) or greater diversity (in intra-cluster evaluations of few-shot generation)~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}.

    \item \textbf{Naive Augmentation Score (NAS)}

    Naive Augmentation Score (NAS) is a measure that was proposed to gauge the contribution of synthetic data towards the accuracy of classification models. It is different from conventional evaluation measures that only look at the accuracy of the model. It determines how effective a classifier model that has used real and synthetic data is in predicting on a real test set. It gives an indication of the usefulness of synthetic data in real applications \citep{ravuri2019classification}. Formally, NAS is defined as:
    \[
    \text{NAS} = \text{Accuracy}_{\text{augmented}} - \text{Accuracy}_{\text{baseline}}
    \]
    where:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\text{Accuracy}_{\text{augmented}}$ is the accuracy of a classifier trained on a combination of real and synthetic data.
        \item $\text{Accuracy}_{\text{baseline}}$ is the accuracy of a classifier trained solely on real data.
    \end{itemize}

    A greater value, including positive ones, of NAS shows that the insert of synthetic data improved the accuracy of the model, a measure that signals the synthetic data is useful to the task. Similarly, a lower value, including negative ones, of NAS shows that the synthetic data did not add to the model's accuracy and might have reduced the accuracy of the model, becoming ineffective \cite{ravuri2019classification}.
    
    \item \textbf{Mutual Information Gap (MIG)}

    MIG is a measure used to assess the extent of disentanglement in the latent representations learned by generative models, especially Variational Autoencoders (VAEs). Disentangled representations seek to isolate various generative factors of variation in different latent dimensions, improving model understandability and control. MIG is used to measure the extent to which a particular generative factor is represented by a single latent variable as opposed to becoming entangled in multiple variables \citep{chen2018isolating}.

    Formally, MIG is defined as:
    \[
    \text{MIG} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{H(v_k)} \left( I(z_{j^{(k)}}; v_k) - \max_{j \neq j^{(k)}} I(z_j; v_k) \right)
    \]
    where:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $K$ is the number of ground truth generative factors.
        \item $v_k$ denotes the $k$-th generative factor.
        \item $z_j$ is the $j$-th latent variable.
        \item $I(z_j; v_k)$ is the mutual information between $z_j$ and $v_k$.
        \item $j^{(k)}$ is the index of the latent variable most informative about $v_k$.
        \item $H(v_k)$ is the entropy of the factor $v_k$, used for normalization.
    \end{itemize}

    Higher MIG score translates to better disentangled representations where each generative factor is represented mainly by a single latent dimension. Lower MIG score would mean that the knowledge of a factor is spread over more latent variables, which is poor disentanglement \citep{locatello2019challenging}.
    
    \item \textbf{Few-Shot Classification Accuracy}
    
    Few-shot accuracy measures the classification performance of a model when only a limited number of labeled examples per class are available. It is typically evaluated under the $N$-way $K$-shot learning setup, where the model is trained on $K$ examples from each of $N$ classes and tested on new samples from the same $N$ classes. The formula is:
    
    \[
    \text{Accuracy} = \left( \frac{\text{Number of Correct Predictions on Query Set}}{\text{Total Number of Query Samples}} \right) \times 100
    \]

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \textbf{To get a reliable estimate:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Sample many episodes (i.e., random $N$-way $K$-shot classification tasks).
        \item Average the accuracy across episodes.
    \end{itemize}
    
    Higher few-shot accuracy indicates better generalization to new tasks from limited examples, which is the core objective in few-shot learning.  
    
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baselines for Comparison}
To contextualize the performance of our proposed few-shot image generation model, we will compare it against a set of strong, widely accepted baseline methods. These baselines represent different paradigms of generative modeling and are selected to provide a fair and comprehensive comparison.

    % \item GMN \citep{pmlr-v84-bartunov18a} – A matching-based generative model that uses attention to condition generation on few-shot support sets without fine-tuning.
    
    \item DAWSON \citep{liang2020dawsondomainadaptiveshot} – A meta-learning framework that adapts GANs across domains using a modified training scheme compatible with any GAN architecture.
    
    % \item DAGAN \citep{antoniou2018dataaugmentationgenerativeadversarial} – A class-agnostic GAN trained to augment datasets by generating diverse intra-class samples from a single input image.
    
    \item MatchingGAN \citep{hong2020matchingganmatchingbasedfewshotimage} – A few-shot GAN that matches support image features with generated outputs via a feature-fusion generator and discriminator.
    
    \item F2GAN \citep{hong2020f2ganfusingandfillingganfewshot} – Enhances MatchingGAN by fusing global support features and refining local textures through non-local attention for more diverse generation.
    
    \item DeltaGAN \citep{hong2022deltagandiversefewshotimage} – Learns transformation deltas between class instances and applies them to generate new images from limited inputs.
    
    \item AGE \citep{AGE} – Leverages latent space attribute editing on pretrained GANs to produce novel-class images without retraining, by disentangling class-relevant and irrelevant features.
    
    \item SAGE \citep{ding2023stableattributegroupediting} – Builds on AGE by aggregating support images to create stable latent embeddings and inject class-consistent diversity via attribute mixing.
\end{itemize}
