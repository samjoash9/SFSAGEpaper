\section{Data Preprocessing}


\subsection{Data Sources}

This research draws from a range of publicly available datasets with a varied scope of visuals such as human faces and flowers. The datasets are popular for generating and attribute-editing applications because of their quality and well-labeled categories. For the purpose of this research's emphasis on few-shot image synthesis, only a minority of the images from each dataset are employed. Specifically, a minimal set of representative samples per class are sampled to replicate the few-shot learning situation. The datasets used are:

The dataset is comprised of 15,803 512~\(\times\)~512 resolution high-quality animal face images and is organized in three domains: cat, dog, and wildlife, with each contributing around 5,000. Released initially as the Animal FacesHQ (AFHQ) dataset, it was specifically intended to facilitate challenging image-to-image translation tasks with a diverse set of breeds (no less than eight per domain) and well-aligned facial features. The photos were gathered from openly available sources under permissive licenses like Flickr and Pixabay. Each photo was curated manually to have good quality, and all had their vertical and horizontal alignments such that the eyes are positioned in the middle which maintains consistency throughout the entire dataset. 500 per domain out of the 15,000 photos were kept for testing, with the rest used as training material. The diversity across species and the standardized preparation of the images make AFHQv2 a solid benchmark for testing generative models, particularly in few-shot or attribute-editing contexts \citep{choi2020starganv2diverseimage}.

     Figure~\ref{fig:VGGFace2} shows sample photographs from the VGGFace2 dataset, which is made up of about 3.3 million photographs covering 9,131 identities with large pose, age, illumination, and ethnic diversity. The dataset was created with the aim of enabling robust face recognition studies through the recording of intra-class variation under natural conditions. The dataset comes with metadata for each of its 2,622 base identities in the form of text records with the URLs of the images and respective face detection coordinates. The annotations facilitate the maintenance of consistent face cropping and cropping positions over a series of photographs. The origin of all the photographs is under suitable usage licenses, and the authors supply pretrained models and implementation notes—ranging from face detection pipelines—through the VGG Face Descriptor website. The size and diversity of the dataset make the benchmark popular for testing face-related tasks such as few-shot face synthesis and identity-preserving face editing. The typical processed image size employed in the VGGFace2 pipeline is 224×224 pixels. It is the size of the central crop obtained after a resizing of the bounding box of the face so that the shorter side is 256 pixels \citep{Parkhi15}.

The flowers covered by the dataset are regularly occurring species in the UK. The dataset shows a great deal of variation in the sizes, pose, and lighting conditions of the flowers, which is an added complexity for the task of classification. Moreover, there are large intra-class differences for some categories, and there are many categories that are visually extremely similar to each other. All such attributes make the dataset extremely suitable for fine-grained visual categorization. The dataset was also used for analysis and visualization using isomap dimensionality reduction based on shape and color descriptors, which revealed the structure of the different flower categories. As for the sizes of the images, the raw images in the dataset are of varying sizes. For instance, one raw image from the dataset measures 500×667 pixels \citep{Nilsback08}.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing Steps}

For all datasets, we apply the following preprocessing steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Resizing and Normalization: Images are resized to 256×256 for efficiency (and 512×512 for high-res experiments) and pixel values are normalized to the range [-1,1] for input to StyleGAN2.
    \item Filtering: We removed any extremely low-quality or mislabeled images.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For VGGFace2 (face data) we use a face detector, MTCNN (Multi-Task Cascaded Convolutional Networks),  to crop and horizontally align faces so the eyes are leveled.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Train-Test Splitting : We assess our approach on four standard few‐shot image domains, partitioning each into “seen” classes for model fitting and “unseen” classes for testing:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Flowers.} We assign 85 categories to the training (seen) split and reserve 15 categories for the test (unseen) split.
      \item \textbf{VGGFaces.} From VGGFaces, we randomly draw 1,800 identities for training and set aside 600 identities for evaluation.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Augmentation: No data augmentation is used beyond centering and flipping, since we focus on the evaluation of given domains. All datasets and preprocessing pipelines are documented for reproducibility.
\end{itemize}
